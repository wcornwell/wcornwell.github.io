Three main lines of work are running in the lab at the moment.

## Biodiversity-friendly renewables

Australia is building wind and solar quickly, and it needs to — climate change is
already the dominant threat to many species here. But the renewable transition
reshapes landscapes, and those changes land on native plants and animals. We are
asking two questions: **where** is renewable development happening relative to
biodiversity, and **what** are the impacts and how can they be mitigated?

On the first, we assembled a national dataset of every utility-scale wind and
solar farm in Australia — 113 wind and 149 solar facilities above 3 MW — and
overlaid it on biome, vegetation, climate, and forest-cover data. Development
clusters near the grid and the population, which means it concentrates in
already-cleared eucalypt forest and woodland: country under pressure from other
land uses already.

On the second, we screened 4,303 studies down to 331 field studies of wind and
solar farm biodiversity impacts worldwide. The evidence is overwhelmingly from
North America and Europe. Wind studies are dominated by bird and bat mortality;
solar studies by ground-layer plants, insects, and under-panel restoration.
Siting and micrositing are the most recommended mitigations, curtailment the most
tested. Crucially, the northern-hemisphere lessons transfer imperfectly: the bats
most affected there are seasonal migrants, while ours are nomads following
erratic eucalypt flowering, which is far harder to predict.

We are now filling the Australian evidence gap directly, with acoustic monitoring
and bird surveys at a wind farm in central-west New South Wales, and working with
the NSW Biodiversity Assessment Method team to standardise how birds and bats are
assessed on wind farms.

**People:** [Ruby Stephens](https://rubyestephens.com), Malan Bothma\
**Collaborators:** Richard Kingsford, Iberdrola Austrralia, NSW DEECCW BAM team

## Machine learning for ecology

Ecology now collects data far faster than anyone can label it: digitised
herbarium sheets, continuous acoustic recordings, camera traps, satellite
imagery. The bottleneck is no longer observation, it is measurement. This strand
of the lab's work builds and applies machine learning methods that turn those raw
archives into ecological measurements at a scale that changes which questions are
answerable.

The clearest example is Karina Guo's work on eucalypt leaves. Rather than
measuring leaves by hand, she built a workflow that extracts leaf area directly
from digitised herbarium sheets of *Eucalyptus*, *Angophora* and *Corymbia* —
**136,599 measurements**, roughly a fifty-fold expansion of the leaf area data
available for the clade.

That scale made a subtle evolutionary pattern visible. Across the clade, leaf
area rises with mean annual temperature and precipitation, matching the
relationship reported for the global flora. But those relationships do not hold
consistently *within* species, plausibly because gene flow suppresses local
adaptation. Looking across phylogenetic scales, the slope of the trait–climate
association converges on the clade-wide slope at surprisingly shallow depths —
around 5–12 million years — suggesting the effect of gene flow relaxes just above
the species level. The paper won the *Journal of Ecology*
[2024 Harper Prize](https://jecologyblog.com/2025/03/27/karina-guo-using-machine-learning-to-link-climate-phylogeny-and-leaf-area-in-eucalypts-through-a-50-fold-expansion-of-leaf-trait-datasets/)
for the best early-career paper of the year.

> Guo, K., Cornwell, W.K. & Bragg, J.G. (2024) Using machine learning to link
> climate, phylogeny and leaf area in eucalypts through a 50-fold expansion of
> leaf trait datasets. *Journal of Ecology*, 112, 2183–2197.
> [10.1111/1365-2745.14354](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2745.14354)

The same toolkit runs through other work in the lab — including the song-meter
analysis in the renewables project above, where models identify birds and
flying-foxes from continuous audio.

**People:** Karina Guo, Jason Bragg (Research Centre for Ecosystem Resilience, Botanic Gardens of Sydney)

## Brown food webs in arid ecosystems

Most thinking about desert ecosystems centres on the "green pulse" of plant
growth that follows rain. But deserts are dry most of the time, and many species
persist — some even thrive — through droughts, when almost nothing is
photosynthesising. Where does that energy come from?

We think the answer is the brown food web: the slow transfer of energy from dead
plant material to invertebrate detritivores, mainly termites, and the predators
that eat them. Grazing disrupts this. Across arid Australia, more than 75 million
sheep, 40 million kangaroos and 24 million cattle strip vegetation before it can
senesce into standing litter, which may starve the brown food web of energy just
as the green food web goes quiet.

A five-year ARC Discovery Project based at UNSW's Fowlers Gap research station
is testing this directly. We are using satellite time series to separate green
and brown vegetation pulses at landscape scale, litter-decomposition experiments
that isolate the roles of termites, UV and microbes under wet and dry
conditions, and large termite-suppression plots (paired with existing herbivore
exclosures) that track the flow-on effects to arthropods, small vertebrates, and
their diets — reconstructed via DNA metabarcoding — as rainfall cycles between
boom and bust.

**People:** Baptiste Wijas, Charlotte Hogan\
**Collaborators:** Mike Letnic, Adrian Fisher, Heloise Gibb, Kylie Cairns
